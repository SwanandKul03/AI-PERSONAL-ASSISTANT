01

Overview

The product is a personal planning assistant for iPhone. You tell it what you need to do, and it turns that into tasks, events and reminders. A short assessment builds a personality profile, which it uses to shape its suggestions.

I worked on it as a UX design consultant. I was given the existing Figma designs and asked to improve them, with freedom to change flows and interactions as well as visuals. Once screens were built, I checked them against my designs and followed up with developers on anything that didn’t match.

02

Understanding the product

Everything in the product feeds one loop: you say what you need, the assistant turns it into something you can act on, and it lands in your plan.

01 →
Talk
Type what’s on your mind, in your own words.
02 →
Turn it into items
The assistant creates tasks, events, goals and reminders.
03 →
See it in your plan
Everything lands on the calendar, alongside connected calendars.
04 ↺
Personalise
An assessment builds a profile that shapes suggestions.

Two moments decide whether that loop works. The first few minutes, when the assistant has to explain itself and learn about you. And every day after, when you open the calendar to see what’s next. My work covered both, plus the prompt in between.

03

The starting point

I started from the existing Figma designs and a UX audit of the app written by someone else, and worked from its findings. These are the ones my redesign addressed.

From the audit · first use
—The intro screens described the company and its philosophy, not what the app does for you.
—Login and sign-up looked almost the same, which caused confusion.
—Password requirements weren’t shown.
—Form errors appeared inline and again in a snackbar.
From the audit · everyday planning
—Switching calendar views was hidden in a dropdown.
—A large date dominated the event list, while titles and times felt secondary.
—Coloured lines and titles on cards had no obvious meaning.
—Events without an objective just said “Not added”.

The audit also covered the dashboard, tasks, goals, settings and the personality report. This case study only shows the parts I designed.

Key design work

All screens are rebuilt with fictional data.

Chapter 1 of 3
First meeting: onboarding and account access

Benefits first, brand story later

Before
The intro screens told the company’s story and philosophy.
Change
I rewrote them around what the app does for you, one benefit per screen, ending on a clear way to create an account or log in.
Why
The intro is where people decide whether the app is worth setting up. A concrete benefit answers that; a brand story doesn’t.DRAFT

Meeting the assistant

The assistant introduces itself before it asks anything: what it will ask, how your answers are used, and how it should talk to you. The assessment then runs as a chat.

Why
This is the first conversation people have with the product. Running the assessment as a chat shows how they’ll use the assistant every day, while giving it what it needs to personalise.DRAFT

Sign-up and login that don’t look alike

Before
The two screens looked almost the same. Password rules weren’t shown, and errors appeared twice: inline and in a snackbar.
Change
Each screen has its own title, supporting line and header, so you know which one you’re on. Sign-up lists the password rules and ticks them off as you type, with a strength meter. Errors appear once, under the field they belong to.
Why
Landing on the wrong screen is a dead end for someone new. Showing the rules up front lets people get the password right first time, and one message in one place is easier to act on.DRAFT

Recovery and success

Change
Forgot password uses the login layout, with a left-aligned heading, so it reads as part of the same flow. After sign-up, a success screen confirms the account and says what happens next.
Why
Both moments need reassurance: you’re still in the right place, and it worked.DRAFT
Chapter 2 of 3
Everyday planning: the calendar

A day you can read at a glance

Before
Views were hidden in a dropdown, and colours on cards had no clear meaning.
Why
On a calendar, position and size already carry meaning. Giving colour one job, status, lets people read a day without opening each event.DRAFT

Moving between dates

Why
Once you leave today it’s easy to lose your place. One tap brings you back.DRAFT

A list that starts with today

Before
A large date dominated the list, while titles and times felt secondary. Events without an objective said “Not added”.
Change
Title and time lead each card, and the date moves into the group heading.
Why
“Not added” describes a gap. “Add objective” tells you what to do about it.DRAFT

Event preview and details

Change
Tapping an event opens a read-only preview. Modify opens the full form.
Why
Most of the time people want to check an event, not edit it. A read-only preview makes accidental changes less likely.DRAFT
Chapter 3 of 3
Talking to the assistant

A prompt that takes only the space it needs

The prompt opens from the centre of the tab bar and stays compact until there’s a conversation.

Why
The prompt sits on top of your plan, so it shouldn’t cover more of it than it has to.DRAFT

Creating by typing

In the create screen, you can type a sentence instead of filling in each field.

Why
Seeing the filled-in form makes it clear what the assistant did with your words.DRAFT

09

Outcomes

The redesign shipped in the iOS app.

I don’t have post-launch data, so I’m not claiming numbers. This case study shows the design reasoning, not measured results.

What changed for people using it
—New users see what the app does before they’re asked to sign up.
—Sign-up and login are clearly different, with password rules and errors shown where they apply.
—The calendar has a visible view switch, a Today shortcut, status lines, duration-sized cards and a current-time line.
—The prompt and in-form creation show what the assistant did with your words.
10

Reflection

DRAFT · REWRITE IN YOUR WORDS

With a personal assistant, the interaction is only half the experience. People also need to understand what it will do with their words and find what it creates. Most of my work was about those moments: explaining the product, getting people in, and making the results of a conversation easy to see and check.
